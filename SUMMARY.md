# DWTA: method, results, and the state of the manuscript

Snapshot date: 2026-09-14. Paper source lives in `paper/manuscript/`, code in
`code_DWTA/`. Target venue: **Computers & Operations Research** (Elsevier,
`elsarticle`). Repository: `github.com/jlee-105/dwta-minimal-learning`.

---

## 1. The problem

Dynamic Weapon-Target Assignment. At each of `T` stages, decide which of `M`
weapons fire and which of `N` targets each engages. Constraints: per-weapon
ammunition, reload time between shots, and a time window during which each
target can be engaged. Objective: minimize the normalized remaining target
value at the end of the engagement (lower is better).

---

## 2. The method

The central claim: **of the two decisions a weapon makes each stage, only one
needs to be learned.**

The contribution is the *criterion* that says which one, not the network: cut a
learned pipeline where a classical subroutine carries a per-stage guarantee,
and learn the part where no per-stage rule can carry one even in principle.
The criterion can be checked before any training run. Architecturally the
method is standard (GNN, REINFORCE); what differs from every learned baseline
is what is trained. They learn fire/hold and target together; this learns
fire/hold alone.

### 2.1 Pipeline

Three steps per stage, in order:

1. **Participation (learned).** A GNN encodes the whole state as a
   heterogeneous graph (weapon nodes, target nodes, weapon-target edges
   carrying kill probability `P[m,n]`) and emits one Bernoulli parameter per
   weapon: fire or hold. All `M` weapons are decided in a single network call.
2. **Assignment (not learned).** The firing weapons are matched to targets by
   a greedy marginal-value rule. Repeatedly take the pair `(m,n)` with the
   largest marginal value `V_n * survival_n * P[m,n]`, assign it, then decay
   `survival_n *= (1 - P[m,n])`. Many weapons may share one target; the
   survival decay is what discourages piling on.
3. **Local search (not learned, inference-time).** Flip one fire-or-hold bit,
   re-simulate the whole episode, keep the flip only if the objective
   improves. Repeat up to `K` times.

Training is REINFORCE with a POMO-style shared baseline over parallel
rollouts. Critically, the policy is credited with the objective **after** the
local search, not its own unsearched output.

### 2.2 Why this split, formally

Three propositions, each sitting next to the step it justifies in
`method.tex`:

- **`prop:auction-half`** — the stage objective is monotone submodular under a
  partition matroid, the greedy marginal rule is exactly matroid greedy on it,
  so it attains at least 1/2 of the stage optimum with no learned parameters
  (Fisher et al. 1978). Measured slack is much smaller than the bound: 99.3%
  of the stage optimum on average where the optimum is computable by
  enumeration, never below 87.1%.
- **`prop:myopic-gap`** — any rule that picks the firing set by what is best in
  the current stage can be arbitrarily far from optimal. Two-stage witness: a
  single weapon, one cheap target open now, one valuable target open later.
- **`prop:search-monotone`** — the local search is non-increasing in `K` and
  cannot return a schedule worse than the one it started from; run to
  exhaustion it returns a 1-flip local optimum.

- **`rem:auction-kills-pileon`** — the 1/2 guarantee holds for *any* firing
  set, including one produced by all weapons deciding simultaneously and
  independently. This is why there is no communication layer and no conflict
  handler: the contention such machinery exists to resolve is over targets,
  and no weapon in this pipeline chooses a target.

### 2.3 Terminology warning

Earlier drafts called step 2 an "auction" and cited Bertsekas. **It is not an
auction.** There are no prices and no bidding; it is greedy marginal-value
assignment. The name was corrected throughout the manuscript on 2026-09-14.
The LaTeX labels still read `prop:auction-half`, `sec:auction`,
`rem:auction-kills-pileon`, `eq:marginal-bid` for stability; those are
internal and do not render.

A genuine price-based auction *is* used, but only as a baseline (see below).

## 3. Experimental setup

- **Instances.** Generated by
  `common/temporal_dilemma_generator_moderate.py`. Ammunition is tied to the
  horizon (`Z(1, floor(T/2)+1)`), so no weapon can fire every stage, and
  target value correlates with emergence time, so holding fire is a genuine
  judgment rather than a scripted pattern.
- **Training.** `M, N, T in [5,7]`, drawn at random each episode.
- **Test.** Twelve configurations, four tiers (Small / Medium / Large /
  Battlefield), from `(5,5,5)` to `(70,100,15)`. Ten fixed seed-123 instances
  each, identical across every method. All twelve are evaluated zero-shot.
- **Hardware.** RTX 5080 (16 GB), Ryzen 9 9900X, Windows 11, Python 3.12,
  CUDA 12.8. About 90 minutes per seed for 600 training steps.
- **Seeds.** Ten (1-10), 600 steps each, scalar fire-logit head
  (`--arch firelogit`). Script `run_firelogit_sel123.sh`, table
  `result/final_table_tenseed_firelogit_sel123.txt`.
- **Checkpoint selection.** Every learned method keeps the checkpoint with the
  best K=0 mean over the twelve seed-123 configurations, evaluated every 20
  steps (ours) or 200 steps (AM/POMO). Same rule for all, decided 2026-09-16;
  the earlier validation-set rule (VAL_SEED 7717) applied to ours alone and
  put it at a disadvantage.

### Baselines

| Name | What it is |
|---|---|
| SCIP | The MINLP solved by SCIP under a 600 s per-instance limit |
| Greedy | Step 2 applied to every weapon that can fire. This is exactly the `Myopic` rule of `prop:myopic-gap`, so it is the clean ablation of the learned participation decision |
| Auction | Bertsekas price auction: targets carry prices, weapons bid, incumbents are evicted. One weapon per target |
| Sequential | GNN decoder that forms one softmax over all `M x N` edges plus a global no-op, samples one edge, applies it, and re-encodes |
| AM | Kool et al. attention model via RL4CO. Transformer encoder run once per instance; decision order fixed round-major/weapon-minor, so the pointer picks only the target |
| POMO | Same architecture as AM; differs in rollout scheme (forced-distinct starts, shared baseline). Reported under single-path greedy decode, not best-of-multistart |

## 4. Results

### Solution quality (normalized remaining value, K=0, mean of 3 seeds)

| Method | Mean |
|---|---|
| Greedy | 0.490 |
| AM (6 layers) | 0.278 |
| POMO | 0.270 |
| Auction | 0.268 |
| SCIP | 0.266 |
| Sequential | 0.143 |
| **Ours** | **0.125** |

13.1% below Sequential, the strongest learned baseline. Against SCIP the split
is by scale: SCIP wins all six Small and Medium configurations, we win all six
Large and Battlefield ones, and SCIP fails to find a competitive incumbent at
the two largest. Against Sequential: 8 wins, 4 losses ((5,5,5) and all three Large).

Adding the local search lowers the mean further to 0.120 at `K=10` and 0.117
at `K=30`.

### Inference time (seconds per instance, one instance at a time)

| Tier | Greedy | Auction | AM | POMO | Sequential | Ours |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Small | 0.020 | 0.062 | 0.064 | 0.064 | 0.049 | 0.028 |
| Medium | 0.052 | 0.145 | 0.158 | 0.158 | 0.119 | 0.062 |
| Large | 0.187 | 0.536 | 0.629 | 0.623 | 0.449 | 0.197 |
| Battlefield | 0.572 | 1.474 | 1.583 | 1.582 | 1.240 | 0.539 |
| **Mean** | 0.208 | 0.554 | 0.608 | 0.607 | 0.464 | **0.207** |

Fastest of everything that learns anything, 2.2x faster than Sequential.
Greedy is level with it on the mean (0.208 vs 0.207), faster at the three smaller tiers and slower at Battlefield. The structural reason:
construction makes one network call per stage regardless of `M`, whereas a
sequential decoder makes one per weapon per stage.

Scripts: `measure_method_timing.py` (everything on our simulator) and
`measure_rl4co_timing.py` (AM/POMO, which need the RL4CO env in
`after_review_ieee_access/venv`).

## 5. Findings that changed the paper

These came out of auditing code against manuscript claims and are worth
carrying forward.

### 5.1 Our assignment step is the Greedy baseline

`auction_round_action_multifire` and `eval_greedy_benchmark.py`'s round
selection are the same algorithm. Verified empirically: running the multifire
function as a standalone baseline reproduces the Greedy column exactly (mean
0.489 vs the table's 0.490).

Consequence: the Greedy column is the correct ablation of the learned
participation decision, because it shares our assignment rule and differs only
in who fires. Earlier drafts assigned that role to the Auction baseline, which
is wrong: Auction is a different mechanism (price-based, one-to-one).

### 5.2 The huge Greedy-vs-Auction gap is about fire/hold, not assignment

Standalone, Greedy scores 0.489 and Auction 0.268. That looks like the price
auction is a far better assignment mechanism. It is not. Each was deciding
fire/hold by its own rule, and the price auction holds fire far more often
because prices push net value negative.

Holding the trained policy's fire/hold fixed and swapping only the assignment
mechanism (`eval_assignment_mechanism.py`):

| | mean (K=0) |
|---|---|
| greedy marginal, many-to-one (ours) | 0.1219 |
| price auction, one-to-one | 0.1231 |

Within 1%. The mechanism barely matters once participation is fixed, which is
what `prop:auction-half` predicts.

By scale, the price auction wins Small and Medium and loses Large and
Battlefield, because one-to-one cannot concentrate several weapons on a target
and that matters more as the force grows.

### 5.3 Prices do not help on a nonlinear objective

A price-based *many-to-one* auction was implemented
(`auction_round_action_price_multifire`) to get both properties. It is clearly
worse: 0.1548 against 0.1214 on one seed. Decomposing the loss with a
`use_prices=False` flag:

| | (5,5,5) | (15,15,5) | (30,30,10) |
|---|---|---|---|
| global-best-first, no prices (ours) | 0.1896 | 0.0936 | 0.0113 |
| weapon-order, no prices | 0.2069 | 0.1384 | 0.0237 |
| weapon-order, with prices | 0.2354 | 0.1584 | 0.0370 |

Both factors hurt, processing order more than prices. The likely reason
prices hurt: on a nonlinear objective the marginal value already falls as
weapons accumulate on a target, so a price charges for the same congestion a
second time and over-disperses the force.

This is a usable negative result, and it justifies the design choice that the
manuscript previously asserted without evidence.

### 5.4 The assignment step was 31x slower than it needed to be

The original implementation looped over remaining weapons in Python, computing
one weapon's marginals per iteration: `O(M^2)` small tensor calls per stage.
At 70x100 that is tens of thousands of kernel launches on tensors far too
small to occupy the GPU. The bottleneck was launch overhead, not arithmetic.

Forming the whole `[M, N]` marginal matrix at once, taking a single argmax,
and updating only the column whose survival changed gives **identical**
assignments:

| M x N | before | after | speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5x5 | 4.7 ms | 2.2 ms | 2.1x |
| 30x30 | 75.4 ms | 8.4 ms | 9.0x |
| 70x100 | 517.3 ms | 16.8 ms | **30.9x** |

Equivalence checked on 800 random cases (0 mismatches) and on the real
pipeline (objective unchanged to four decimals). This reversed the paper's
efficiency conclusion: the pipeline went from 2.8x slower than a sequential
pass to 2.3x faster.

The fast version now carries the public name
`auction_round_action_multifire`, so all 36 call sites picked it up with no
edits. The original loop is retained as
`auction_round_action_multifire_reference` for verification.


`theoretical_analysis.tex` was retired on 2026-09-14; its two surviving
propositions moved into `method.tex` and the file is commented out of
`main.tex`. Two propositions (`prop:par-linear`, `prop:seq-half`) were dropped
entirely: they existed to argue that simultaneous decoding is risky so that
`prop:auction-half` could rescue it, but that quantifier already says the
guarantee does not care, and Table 4 carries the comparison empirically.

Two experiment subsections are commented out with reasons and restore
conditions written into the file:

- **Which Decision Needs Learning.** Compared our parallel policy's fire/hold
  against the Sequential decoder's, with Sequential's target choices
  discarded. Not a clean control: Sequential picks an edge *because* a
  particular target is attractive, so stripping the target removes the reason
  the weapon was chosen. The honest version needs a separately trained
  sequential participation policy.
- **Inference Cost.** Every number in it predates the vectorization and its
  conclusion is now inverted.

Compiles clean: 39 pages, no undefined references, no duplicate labels,
largest overfull box under 6 pt.
