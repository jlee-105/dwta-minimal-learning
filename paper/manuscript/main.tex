\begin{abstract}
The Dynamic Weapon-Target Assignment Problem (DWTA) asks, at every stage of an
engagement, which weapons should fire and which target each should engage,
subject to reload times, ammunition limits, and target availability windows.
Learned methods for this problem train a policy to produce both decisions. We
argue that the two are not alike, and that only one of them should be learned.

What separates them is whether a classical mechanism can be held to a
guarantee. Given the set of weapons firing at a stage, assigning targets among
them is monotone submodular maximization under a partition matroid, so
assigning many-to-one by each weapon's exact marginal value attains at least
half the stage optimum with no learned parameters. That bound holds for any
firing set, including one produced by all weapons deciding at the same
instant, which is why our architecture needs neither a communication layer nor
a conflict handler. Choosing which weapons fire admits no such treatment.
Holding fire is worth nothing within a stage and everything across stages, and
we prove that any rule optimizing the current stage can destroy an arbitrarily
small fraction of what an optimal policy destroys. This yields a criterion for
where to cut a learned pipeline, checkable before any training run: delegate
the part that carries a per-stage guarantee, and learn the part that provably
cannot have one.

Applied to DWTA the criterion leaves one bit per weapon per stage. A graph
neural network emits that bit for every weapon in a single pass; the greedy
rule assigns targets; a fixed local search then revises the schedule and
cannot degrade it. Trained only on instances of 5 to 7 weapons, targets and
stages, the framework generalizes zero-shot to 70 weapons, 100 targets and 15
stages. It outperforms every baseline that learns both decisions, lowering the
mean objective 14.9\% below the strongest of them while running 2.3 times
faster, and the same pipeline with the learned bit removed is four times
worse, which locates the value precisely where the criterion predicts.
